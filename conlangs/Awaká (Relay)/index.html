Awaká (Relay)

Awaká

Updated 21 Sept 2026

Awaká is a language spoken in a tropical forest by nomadic hunter-gatherers, located in the southwestern part of the continent, in a area shared with other peoples but ultimately dominated by speakers of Ngowan languages, which Awaká is part of.

The language is very analytic, mainly SVO and head initial. It has a very small phonology and CV syllable structure, this makes phonemes with a high phonemic load.

Introduction

Society in Awaká is split into tribes called sikí, they are represented by a man called towá (chief), that is elected by the people. Roles in the society are usually fluid, people can in general choose what they do, but men are usually the ones that hunt and fight. The function of the towá is to coordinate attacks by other people and ojimeŋá, and to interact with other sikí for resources.

Every tribe has multiple ŋatá (clan) that are represented by the oldest woman in each family, Awaká is a matrilocal society, meaning men leave their sikí to live in another tribe, so woman are left to do stuff locally.

People build a tribe with a big house for each ŋatá and stay in the area for one or more years until the resources start to deplete, the area of the sikí, also called sikí, is enclosed by palisades to defend against ojimeŋá or other attackers. Houses are built with leaves, wood and mud and have to be maintained constantly, before leaving an area any resource that can be reused is store for when the area is good to return.

They make tools from stone and bones. Foodstuff is made out of flour from tubers, dried grains, oil from plants and some amount of meat. The threat of monsters has decreased the availability of meat, that pushed people to stay in the same place longer, so they started helping plants grow in their area by taking care of the environment around, nobody knows what ojimeŋajá are, hence the name oji "cut" + meŋá "know".

The romanization of Awaká is very straightforward, it is identical to IPA with the stressed syllable, that is always final, marked by an acute.

Consonants

Nasals

The nasals series in Awaká corresponds to consoanants that range from fully nasal to fully oral in other related languages.

The realization of these consonants in Awaká is fully nasal most times, but speakers may occasionally realize them post-oralized.

Obstruents

The plosive consonants are very consistent across all related languages. The central dialect of Awaká has its word final plosives aspirated

The /s/ is a laminal alveolar sibilant and is realized as postalveolar [ʃ] before /u/.

/r/

The /r/ is most often a trill with a single flap, but is variously produced as a longer trill. In atonal environments it can be lenited to a non-sibilant voiced alveolar fricative [ɹ̝] or velar [ɣ] before back vowels.

It can also be nasalized to [n] in sequences of /reN/ with N being any nasal vowel. By observing related languages it's clear the word initial /r/ merged with /n/.

Approximants

The approximants /w j/ are slightly fricated, that is they come close to [ɣʷ ʝ] respectively. Both consonants are used to break vowels in hiatus, but the exact distribution of them is unclear.

A glottal stop found in related languages can correspond to either /w/ or /j/. /j/ is found in frontal environments and /w/ in rounded ones, but they mix when both are present.

Vowels

The vowels /e o/ are greatly restricted compared to /a i u/, for instance /e o/ are only found in unstressed syllables, otherwise they become /i u/ respectively.

/e o/ are also not found before plosives and fricatives in root words but always before nasals, that hints that these vowels are likely a reflex of nasalized vowels. In fact in the East dialect are nasalized before approximants.

But these vowels are also found in many grammatical morphemes and some prefixes that wouldn't be necessarily nasalized.

While the vowels /a i u/ show no notable variation, /e/ and /o/ do:

  • /e/ centralizes to [ɨ] before dorsal consonants
  • the sounds [e o] open to [ɛ ɔ] in words ending in /a/

Syllables

Every syllable is CV except the word initial that can have no consonant. Stress is fixed on the last syllable of content words, most grammatical words have no stress and are phonologically attached to a content word.

Every stressed word has an acute accent ⟨á í ú⟩ in the orthography, and the vowels /e o/ can't be stressed.

When an unstressed word ending in a vowel is followed by a word starting with a vowel, the starting vowel takes over the other one. In the case of stressed words equal vowels become one, different vowels will have an inserted /j/ before /i/ or /w/ otherwise.

Dialects

Awaká is spoken in a somewhat large area and can be considered a dialect continuum, the description here is about the Central dialect. Other relevant varieties of the language are the Coastal dialect and the East dialect. The major difference between these dialects are the realization of the unstressed vowels /e/ and /o/.

The Coastal dialect both vowels merged into a new vowel phoneme /ə/, this dialect brought new words to the Central dialect relating about sea animals and products, the /ə/ vowel there was internally loaned as /e/.

The East dialect the vowels /e o/ are nasalized before approximants and nasals, and sometimes /a/ in the other dialects corresponds with /e/ in this dialect.

Parts of Speech

Awaká has only two contentive parts of speech, nouns and verbs, and verbs have four different types:

  • Dynamic verbs
  • Stative verbs
  • Locative verbs
  • Interrogative verbs

The stative verbs cover many meanings represented by adjectives in other languages.

Locative and interrogative verbs are a very small and closed class, of verbs that diverge slightly for the rules.

Other parts of speech that serve grammatical meaning are: Articles, Prepositions, Conjunctions and particles (like the copula).

Awaká is a very isolating language, the only inflectional suffix is the associative -já. But there is a small number of derivational affixes.

Derivation

The content words have somewhat flexible parts of speech, this is because they can be combined with other deriving words very easily, but they don't zero derive (without any modification) very often.

The most productive form of derivations is turning verbs into nouns with the prefix a-, it seems to be related with the definite article. It can be used to derive agents, objects, instrumentals and other things without precise meaning.

anawá "smell" nawá "to smell"
atisí "food" tisí "to eat"
aŋisá "light" ŋisá "bright"
asenú "part" senú "to cut"
ajitú "voice" itú "to speak"

Another productive prefix is to- used to derive words relating to humans or body parts from other nouns, it has fewer examples than a-. Most of the derived words lost the original stem, examples are tojipú "giant", topewa "man", tojí "hand", towá "leader".

Light Verbs

Some verbs are formed by two words, the first is a light verb and the second can be a noun or another verb. The light verbs are small group of verbs that have little meaning by themselves and combine with other words to make complex verbs.

There are four main light verbs, they are not accented when in a light verb. These verbs are rarely standing alone, with a major exception of nuhí when it represents a generalized action.

nuhi "to do" makes verbs from nouns

Pronouns

In general, pronouns in Awaká are not distinguished by number, but there are associative pronouns those convey relatedness rather than number. Though, the first person has a plural form that is used in contrast with the associative form.

Personal Pronouns

This is the regular set of pronouns, more details about each person are given below.

Normal Associative Plural
1st kú kujá (EXCL) atutú (INCL)
2nd wú wujá
3rd tú tujá

The same set of pronouns is used for all syntactic functions, that is there is no case marking for them. The language is not pro-drop, pronouns cannot be omitted from the sentence.

The associative pronouns mean "(pronoun) and related", for the first person associative pronoun kujá that is interpreted as an exclusive first person (me and my group, not you) but another kind of first person pronoun atutú is an inclusive first person (me and everyone).

Possessive Pronouns

The possessive pronouns are formed by the prefix sa- on the pronouns, directly derived from the sa particle, these pronouns are always dependent on another noun so they are never accented, except the associative. These pronouns must directly follow the noun they modify.

Normal Associative Plural
1st saku sakujá satutu
2nd (sawu) (sawujá)
3rd satu satujá

The possessive second person pronoun, while would follow the pattern, is avoided in speech. This is related with the second person formality phenomenon.

Second Person Formality

The second person pronoun wú has restrictions on its formality. First is only used towards adults, kids are instead referred to by aldults with ahá "son" or with their proper noun, children refer to each other by the words aŋú “girl” and sitá “boy”.

Within members of the same family the use of kinship terms is favored instead of wú. To refer to second person possession a special word called the second person vocative nowú is used.

This vocative sets the topic of the conversation as the other person and functions as social distancing of the other. This same pattern is observed with the other words above but they don't have any special vocative form, they are simply fronted in the sentence.

Demonstrative Pronouns

The demonstrative pronouns have two distance distinctions, a proximal and distal. They can be dependent or independent, with the dependent ones having no accent.

This demonstrative system is person-oriented, that means the proximal pronoun is used for things close either to the speaker or the listener, and the distal for things far from both.

Articles are used to assign some level of specification to nouns, Awaká has two articles.

a Definite a nojá "the house"

Associative Suffix

Nouns in Awaká can take the associative suffix -já, it always stress the word, its fucntion is similar to a plural, but it means "X and related" instead. There is no proper plural marking in Awaká.

Most often the suffix is used with proper nouns, in that case the meaning covers this person and their relatives within a ŋatá, in other contexts it can also refer to friends or a whole tribe.

(1)

“Wenaru and relatives have come”

Applied to kinship terms the associative is much closer to a plural meaning, with regular kinship terms being more specific. For example, compare anú "(my) brother" and anujá "all (my) brothers".

When used with non-human nouns the meaning of the suffix becomes quite broad, instead very often having a collective or complementary meaning. Example of complementary meanings are the following:

uwikí "bow" uwikijá "bow and arrow"

tojí "hand" tojijá "hand and fingers"
wejí "fire" wejijá "fire and wood"

Verbs

Verbs have two main types: dynamic and stative. There are also two other types that are the interrogatives and locatives, they are few verbs that behave a little different and have more restrictions but they are also similar to statives.

Stative verbs are intransitive verbs that describe states of being and are similar in function to adjectives. Dynamic verbs describe actions and may be transitive or intransitive.

There are four 'tenses' in Awaká, they are marked by auxiliary words before the verb, and the simple tense is unmarked.

Simple V kojí "love"
Perfect niri V niri ují "has slept"
Progressive enu V enu omojú "is running"
Future sahi V sahi kojí "will love"

The progressive cannot be used with stative verbs but the perfect and future can.

Verbs can also be in four different voices, voices change the roles of the participants in the syntax. They too are marked by auxiliary words before the verb.

Active V kojí "X loves Y", itú "X says Y"
Passive ke V ke kojí "X is loved"
Applicative no V no itú "X talks with Y"
Causative ja V ja itú "X makes say Y"

The voice markers are always closer to the verb than tense markers.

Voices

The voice markers changes the place of the arguments of a verb, Awaká has 3 voices other than active (the default), the passive, applicative and causative.

The passive marker ke promotes the object to subject and reduces transitive verbs to intransitive.

(1)

(2)

In the example above tikú is promoted from object to subject.

The agent in a passive can be indicated by the preposition ŋo.

(3)

ŋaby_the

“Meat is eaten by the dog”

Only transitive verbs can become passive, because intransitives don't have an object.

The applicative marker no is a bit more complicated, for intransitive verbs it promotes an oblique object (one that would be indicated by a preposition) and turns it into a direct object. For transitive verbs it instead removes the existing object and replaces it by another.

There are many different ways to ask questions in Awaká, most important is the division between polar questions (those can be answered by yes or no) and content questions (those can be answered with anything).

Content questions can be made with the question word wipá or with question verbs.

While wipá covers many cases, some questions are better asked with interrogative verbs instead, they are a special kind of stative verb. These verbs are also moved to the start of the sentence, they replace other wh-words as explained in the table:

utuwá "who" utuwá tú? "who are they?"
ikuwá "when" ikuwá isí? "when is there rain?"
saruwá "where" saruwá tú? "where are they?"
ituwá "say what" ituwá tú? "what they say?"
ná "how" ná tú? "how are they?"

Most of these words are clearly derived from a noun with a suffix -wá, but this is not a productive suffix in the language.

Because all these verbs are moved to the start they might look like are in a VS order, but only the main verb moves, so if the verb has tense markers they will be after the subject.

Comparison

Expressions of comparison are made with the comparative particles posi "more" and masi "less", these words are followed by a standard of comparison that can be either a noun or a stative verb.

Comparatives made from stative verbs are verbs themselves (1), an optional object of comparison is marked with the preposition nu (2). They may as well be used like adjectives that specify a referent (3).

(1)
kú1
posimore
upútall
“I am taller”
(2)
kú1
posimore
upútall
naat_the
urátree
“I am taller than the tree”
(3)
a aháthe son
posimore
upútall
sahifut
wasíwalk
naat_the
ŋikíforest
“The tallest son will go to the forest”

There is no morphological distinction between regular comparatives and superlatives in Awaká, depending on context posi or masi can mean "most" or "least" respectively, that is likely the case for an subject marked as definite (4), another way to be more specific is to use kikí "all" as a standard of comparison (5).

(4)
a uráthe tree
posimore
upútall
“The tallest tree”
(5)
a uráthe tree
masiless
upútall
nuat
kikíall
“The least tall tree (of all)”

The stative verb ŋisí "be better" is also a comparative verb but it doesn't need a comparative particle, but like them it also marks its object of comparison with nu. The standard of comparison is optional, subject to context, but can be specified with sa (6). Like the other comparatives it can also be superlative (7).

(6)
kú1
ŋisíbetter
saof
niwíswim
nuat
Powatúpname
“I am better at swiming than Powatú”
(7)
wú2
ŋisíbetter
saof
niwíswim
“You are the best swimmer”

Comparatives made from nouns create verbs that indicate possession (8), and can be used with nu (9) or as adjectives (10) just like the other comparisons.

(8)
kú1
posimore
ŋirúseed
“I have more seeds” or “I have most seeds”
(9)
kú1
posimore
ŋirúseed
nuat
Powatúpname
“I have more seeds than Powatú”
(10)
a pisáthe fruit
masiless
ŋirúseed
posimore
puwútasty
“The fruits with less seeds are more tasty”
